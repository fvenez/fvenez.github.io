## Science fiction ##

This is a list of science fiction stories and novels I've read that deal significantly with topics related to AI.

Some recurrent themes are:
🤖 Thinking machines | 💥 Intelligence explosion and the Singularity | 🧠 Posthumanism, mind uploading and transcendence | 🔭 Limits of human understanding | ⚖️ Impact on society and the economy


1. Samuel Butler, [*The Book of the Machines*](./assets/texts/Butler - The Book of the Machines.pdf) (in [_Erewhon_](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1906/1906-h/1906-h.htm), 1872) 🤖 ⚖️

    _Erewhon_ is a satirical novel of the "lost world" genre which criticizes the Victorian society; I recommend it in its entirety only to fans of the genre, but chapters 23–25 form a self-contained unit (_The Book of the Machines_) which Butler developed from an earlier 1863 article, _Darwin among the Machines_. These chapters detail how the inhabitants of Erewhon have destroyed their most advanced machines out of fear that, evolving through Darwinian selection, they would end up acquiring consciousness and taking control. For a text from 1872, I found its definition of intelligence incredibly modern. The _Butlerian Jihad_ in the _Dune_ series is a reference to Samuel Butler.



1. Fredric Brown, [*Answer*](https://www.roma1.infn.it/~anzel/answer.html) (1954) 🤖 🧠

    A very short story about the creation of a supercomputer. [Italian translation](https://www.rizzolieducation.it/content/uploads/2022/10/11_brown_10_novembre.pdf).

1. Isaac Asimov, [*The Last Question*](https://archive.org/details/Science_Fiction_Quarterly_New_Series_v04n05_1956-11_slpn/page/n5/mode/2up) (1956) 🤖 🧠

    The story Asimov considered his favorite.

1. Stanisław Lem, *Golem XIV* (1981) 🤖 💥 🔭

    A supercomputer built for military purposes refuses to serve in war and gives a series of lectures to human scientists on the nature of humanity, evolution and intelligence. There is no real narrative: the book consists of the transcripts of the lectures Golem gave before falling silent forever, and of the commentary on them by some human scientists.

1. William Gibson, *Neuromancer* (1984) 🤖 💥

    The quintessential cyberpunk novel. Although the book focuses on a group of hackers, the plot is driven behind the scenes by an AI that is legally prevented from becoming too intelligent. There is even a police force, the _Turing Police_, whose job is precisely to prevent the emergence of overly advanced AIs. It is one of the most famous and influential novels in science fiction. I personally found the writing style rather unpleasant, but that's a matter of taste. Hugo Award 1985.

1. Iain M. Banks, *The Culture series* (1987–2012) 🤖 🧠 💥 ⚖️

    An entire series set in a post-scarcity galactic civilization in which the Minds, vastly superior artificial intelligences, run spaceships and habitats while humans live in a utopia free from want and need. It is the most optimistic version of a post-Singularity future. Banks also explores its darker sides such as widespread ennui or its militant interventionism toward less advanced civilizations. The setting is very interesting, but in my opinion Banks wasn't a very good writer.

1. Dan Simmons, *Hyperion Cantos* (1989–1997) 🤖 🧠 ⚖️

    A space opera in which society has become dependent on artificial intelligences, which turn against it. The setting is original and the books also touch on themes related to the evolution of humanity.
    The TechnoCore (the malevolent AIs) is later linked to [Tierra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tierra_(computer_simulation)), a digital evolution experiment from the early '90s by biologist [Thomas S. Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_S._Ray). There are references to the theological ideas of [Pierre Teilhard de Chardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Teilhard_de_Chardin). The books are very well written; the plot gets somewhat out of hand in the later ones, but I do recommend the first, which has an episodic structure inspired by _The Canterbury Tales_. Hugo Award 1990.

1. Ted Chiang, *Understand* (1991) 💥 🔭

    After an experimental treatment for brain damage, the protagonist develops superhuman intelligence. The story follows his enhancement up to a confrontation with another enhanced individual. An attempt to portray from the inside a mind that surpasses the human one.

1. Vernor Vinge, *A Fire Upon the Deep* (1992) 💥 🧠 🔭

    In this space opera the galaxy is divided into "Zones of Thought", where the laws of physics set a limit on the highest possible intelligence: only in the outer zones can there be transcendent entities incomprehensible to mortals, while in the intermediate bands civilization is closer to the one we know. The novel follows the attempt to stop a runaway super-intelligence. Hugo Award 1993. Highly recommended. I also recommend Vinge's next novel in the same setting, _A Deepness in the Sky_, which gives, in a different guise, a chilling impression of what an AI-powered surveillance state might look like.

1. Greg Egan, *Diaspora* (1997) 🤖 🧠 🔭

    In the 30th century most of humanity lives as software in digital communities, but there are also robots and biological humans. The opening chapter, describing the birth of an artificial mind, is a small masterpiece. The story follows an expedition that leaves Earth after a gamma-ray burst from a nearby star. As many books by Egan, this is rather maths-heavy; I see the "Truth Mines" that appear in the book as one of the possible futures of mathematics. This is a posthuman future with human-level artificial intelligences and without a Singularity.

1. Ted Chiang, [*Catching Crumbs from the Table*](https://www.nature.com/articles/35014679) (2000) 🧠 🔭 ⚖️

    Published in _Nature_ and later collected under the title _The Evolution of Human Science_ in _Stories of Your Life and Others_. In a few pages, written as a scientific article, Chiang imagines a world in which scientific research is carried out by "metahumans", and human scientists are left only with interpreting their discoveries. This seems to be the melancholy future of mathematics.

1. Charles Stross, [*Accelerando*](https://www.antipope.org/charlie/blog-static/fiction/accelerando/accelerando.html) (2005) 🤖 💥 🧠 ⚖️

    A novel built from stories published between 1999 and 2004, following three generations of a family before, during and after the Singularity. The book is fun and has a dizzying pace. It perfectly captures how I (can't) imagine the Singularity: extremely fast, chaotic and incomprehensible. Many of the early details now feel dated because they are closely tied to the optimistic mood of the early 2000s, but I liked this book a lot.


While not directly related to AI, I also want to mention [_The Feeling of Power_](https://www.physics.rutgers.edu/~friedan/physics_readings/Asimov,%20Isaac/Asimov,%20Isaac,%201958,%20%22The%20Feeling%20of%20Power%22.pdf) and [_Profession_](https://www.physics.rutgers.edu/~friedan/physics_readings/Asimov,%20Isaac/Asimov,%20Isaac,%201957,%20%22Profession%22.pdf), by Isaac Asimov, on the theme of deskilling.

## Essays ##

These, on the other hand, are some essays and articles I have read recently. Given how quickly computer capabilities are changing, these texts are mostly of historical interest.

13. I. J. Good, [*Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/bookseries/abs/pii/S0065245808604180) (1965)

    The essay that first rigorously formulated the idea of an "intelligence explosion": a machine capable of designing machines better than itself would start a cycle of recursive self-improvement.

14. Vernor Vinge, [*First Word*](./assets/texts/Vinge - First Word.pdf) (Omni, 1983)

    A short editorial in _Omni_ magazine in which Vinge explicitly uses the term "Singularity" for the creation of intelligences greater than our own. He compares that moment to the center of a black hole: a point beyond which human history becomes impenetrable, and beyond which science fiction writers can no longer imagine. A few lines that gave a name to an idea.

15. Vernor Vinge, [*The Coming Technological Singularity: How to Survive in the Post-Human Era*](./assets/texts/Vinge - How to survive the Singularity.pdf) (1993)

    Vinge predicted that within thirty years we would have the technological means to create superhuman intelligence, and that shortly afterwards the human era would end. He explores several possible paths to the Singularity (AI, computer networks, human-computer interfaces and biological enhancement) and suggests some ways in which humans might survive.

16. Hans Moravec, [*When will computer hardware match the human brain?*](https://www.jetpress.org/volume1/moravec.pdf) (1997)

    Thoughts on the faster-than-exponential growth of available computing power, and estimates of the computing power of the human brain.

17. Ted Chiang, [*Silicon Valley Is Turning Into Its Own Worst Fear*](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/tedchiang/the-real-danger-to-civilization-isnt-ai-its-runaway) (2017)

    A critique of the fear of a runaway super-intelligence. Chiang observes that the behavior of an AI pursuing a single goal ruthlessly and at any cost matches the way big tech companies already behave, and he observes that they are the real problem of our times.

18. Rich Sutton, [*The Bitter Lesson*](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html) (2019)

    Thoughts on the link between available computing power and progress in AI.

19. Ted Chiang, [*No, Artificial Intelligence Is Not Conscious*](https://www.theatlantic.com/philosophy/2026/06/no-artificial-intelligence-is-not-conscious/687378/) (2026)

    A critique of the idea that current language models might be close to some form of consciousness.
